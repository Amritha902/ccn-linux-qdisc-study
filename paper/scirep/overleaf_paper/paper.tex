\title{\vspace{-1.2cm}When Does Adapting a Queue Discipline's Parameters Pay?
A Scaling Law in \texttt{target}/RTT, Measured on Stock Linux}
\author{Amritha S \quad Yugeshwaran P \quad Deepti Annuncia\\[2pt]
\small Department of Electronics and Communication Engineering\\
\small Vellore Institute of Technology, Chennai, India}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Linux ships \texttt{fq\_codel} with four parameters fixed at configuration
time. Whether adapting them at runtime is worth doing has been answered both
ways, and neither answer states when the other holds. Across 178 runs spanning
three workloads, eight alternative queue disciplines and path round-trip times
from 2 to 200\,ms, we find the benefit of adaptation is governed not by path
RTT but by the dimensionless ratio $r=\texttt{target}/\mathrm{RTT}$. The
benefit follows a logistic curve in $r$ with ceiling \LawCeiling\,\% and half
of that reached at $r=\LawHalfBenefit$ ($R^{2}=\LawRsq$), a form that holds
across a 16-fold change in RTT and transfers to \texttt{codel} and PIE.
Published guidance places a correctly configured deployment at $r$ between
0.05 and 0.10, so half the available benefit lies five to ten times outside
that envelope: adaptation is a remedy for misconfiguration rather than an
improvement on correct configuration. Two measurements frame the result.
Flow queueing with a delay target cuts tail latency 99.0\,\% below an
unmanaged queue at indistinguishable goodput, two orders of magnitude more
than any tuning measured here; and CAKE, which needs no controller, beats the
adapted system at every RTT tested. All hypotheses and decision thresholds
were registered before the data existed.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{active queue management; bufferbloat; \texttt{fq\_codel}; CoDel;
dimensionless scaling law; runtime parameter adaptation; Linux \texttt{tc};
pre-registered evaluation}

\section{Introduction}

Bufferbloat is the inflation of queueing delay by buffers larger than any
control loop requires, while throughput stays unaffected\cite{gettys2012}.
Linux answers it with \texttt{fq\_codel}: Deficit Round Robin across roughly a
thousand flow queues, each with its own CoDel instance\cite{rfc8290}. Four
parameters govern it and none changes once set: \texttt{target} (5\,ms),
\texttt{interval} (100\,ms), \texttt{limit} and \texttt{quantum}.

Adapting those parameters at runtime has clear precedent. Adaptive RED adjusts
a drop probability by an AIMD rule\cite{ared2001}; Ye and Leung adapt CoDel's
interval analytically\cite{yeleung2020}; DESiRED adapts an AQM's delay target
in P4 with deep reinforcement learning\cite{desired2024}. Runtime adaptation
of a delay target is therefore not novel and we claim no novelty for it.

What the literature does not supply is the condition under which adaptation
pays. Systems report gains; independent evaluations report none; neither camp
states when the other is right. RFC 7567 meanwhile recommends AQM that needs
no per-flow configuration\cite{rfc7567}, which makes a null result for
adaptation a reportable outcome rather than a failed experiment.

This paper establishes that condition experimentally, and it turns out to be a
dimensionless ratio rather than any absolute quantity. Section~\ref{s:rel}
places the work against the base paper and six recent systems,
Section~\ref{s:method} describes the apparatus, Section~\ref{s:res} gives the
measurements, and Section~\ref{s:law} derives and tests the law.

%% =====================================================================
\section{Related work}\label{s:rel}

Random Early Detection opened the field, dropping with a probability derived
from average queue length\cite{red1993}; its sensitivity to configuration
prompted Adaptive RED\cite{ared2001}, whose AIMD policy and $\beta=0.9$
decrease factor our controller adopts. PIE replaced queue length with a delay
estimate inside a PI controller\cite{rfc8033}. CoDel abandoned queue length
entirely, dropping when minimum sojourn time over a sliding interval exceeds a
target\cite{nichols2012,rfc8289}; its authors argue the parameters are
RTT-relative by construction and need no tuning. Equation~\ref{eq:law}
quantifies where that argument stops holding. CAKE integrates shaping, flow
queueing and host fairness\cite{cake2018}, FQ-PIE is the corresponding
combination for PIE\cite{fqpie2019}, and L4S standardises a low-latency class
on a dual-queue coupled AQM\cite{rfc9330,rfc9332}. All eight disciplines are
measured here under one identical bottleneck.

Table~\ref{t:sys} places this work against the base paper and six recent
systems. Two things follow. Adapting a delay target at runtime is established,
so the mechanism is not our contribution. And no entry states the condition
under which its adaptation pays, which is what we supply.

Two pieces of apparatus are missing throughout. A controller that merely
occupies the CPU is hard to tell from one that helps unless a third arm runs
the same code and applies nothing, and the sparse probe is usually averaged
into the bulk flows even where the discipline was built to treat them
differently. Without both, a small difference cannot be attributed to
anything.

\section{Method}\label{s:method}

Three network namespaces model a client, a router and a server. The router
carries \texttt{netem} for base delay and a token-bucket shaper at 10\,Mbit/s
as root qdisc, with the discipline under test as its child on the egress
\emph{toward the server}, so the bottleneck sits on the data path. An earlier
two-node arrangement shaped the reverse direction, and every backlog figure
taken that way measured acknowledgements rather than data.

Nine configurations are compared: \texttt{pfifo}, SFQ, RED, \texttt{codel},
PIE, FQ-PIE, CAKE, static \texttt{fq\_codel}, and \texttt{fq\_codel} under our
controller. A tenth, the \emph{sham} controller, polls at the controller's
cadence and runs the same classification and prediction code but applies no
change, which separates the controller's CPU cost from its decisions.

The controller reads \texttt{tc} statistics and per-flow state from an eBPF
program at the \texttt{tc} clsact egress hook, classifies a congestion regime,
estimates a trajectory by least squares over a ten-sample window, and applies
an AIMD adjustment. Observation and update run on separated timescales, an
order of magnitude apart, following two-timescale stochastic
approximation\cite{borkar1997}. Every bound and step size is expressed
relative to the configured \texttt{target}, not in absolute milliseconds;
expressed absolutely, a controller started against an 80\,ms target would take
steps 16 times smaller in relative terms than against 5\,ms, and the measured
effect would then be an artefact of the constants.

Three latencies are measured separately: a sparse ICMP probe, bulk TCP flow
RTT reported by \texttt{iperf3}, and a queue-delay proxy derived from backlog
and rate. Workloads are a steady eight-flow bulk load, a staged load whose
flow count varies from two to twenty-four during the run, and a mixed load
adding sparse UDP probes. Each cell is repeated three times. Differences use
Welch's $t$-test with exact two-sided $p$-values from the regularised
incomplete beta function, since at three samples per group the normal
approximation materially overstates significance. The transport is TCP
CUBIC\cite{cubic2008} on Linux 6.12.48 with every discipline compiled in.

\section{Results}\label{s:res}

\subsection{The dominant effect is not tuning}

Figure~\ref{f:tail} gives 95th-percentile probe latency for all nine
configurations. The disciplines that combine flow queueing with a delay target
sit within a few milliseconds of one another; the unmanaged queue sits two
orders of magnitude above, at \PfifoProbeRttPninetyfiveSteady\,ms against
\FqCodelProbeRttPninetyfiveSteady\,ms, a 99.0\,\% reduction ($p<0.001$) at
goodput that is statistically indistinguishable (\PfifoGoodputSteady\ against
\FqCodelGoodputSteady\,Mbit/s, $p=0.20$). The static, sham and adapted
\texttt{fq\_codel} points are not separable at this scale, which is the first
indication that parameter tuning is a second-order effect.

Fair queueing alone is not sufficient. SFQ isolates flows but applies no AQM,
and its bulk queues sit at their 127-packet limit throughout: its probe reads
\SfqProbeRttMeanSteady\,ms while the bulk flows sharing the link read
\SfqBulkRttMeanSteady\,ms. The scheduler and the AQM do distinct jobs and both
are necessary.

\section{A scaling law for when adaptation pays}\label{s:law}

The measurements above are taken at one path RTT. Sweeping base RTT from 2 to
200\,ms while holding the configured \texttt{target} fixed shows the benefit
of adaptation is not a function of RTT at all, but of the ratio
$r=\texttt{target}/\mathrm{RTT}$. Fitting bulk-flow RTT reduction $b$ over
\LawCells\ cells gives
%
\begin{equation}
b(r) \;=\; \frac{B}{1 + \left(r_{0}/r\right)^{k}},
\quad
\begin{aligned}
B &= \LawCeiling\,\%\\
r_{0} &= \LawHalfBenefit\\
k &= \LawSteepness
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:law}
\end{equation}
%
with $R^{2}=\LawRsq$, where $B$ is the ceiling, $r_{0}$ the ratio at which half
of it is reached and $k$ the sharpness of the transition
(Figure~\ref{f:law}).

The hypotheses and their decision thresholds were committed to the repository
before the data existed. Three held. A crossover exists: benefit stays
under 1.5\,\% and is not significant below $r=0.25$, and reaches
16.4--\LawPeakBenefit\,\% at $r\ge1$. The curve is invariant across a
16-fold change in RTT: at fixed $r=1$, cells at 5, 20 and 80\,ms give 18.4,
17.6 and 16.4\,\%, a spread of 2.0 percentage points against a 5-point
threshold. Leave-one-out prediction of the interior ratios has a mean absolute
error of 1.4 percentage points, worst case 3.5. The form also transfers to
mechanisms the fit never saw: refitted independently, plain \texttt{codel}
places $r_{0}$ at 0.42 against \texttt{fq\_codel}'s 0.49, and PIE, which
shares nothing with CoDel beyond having a delay target, at 1.06
($R^{2}=0.978$ and $0.992$).

One pre-registered test failed, and we report it. The spread in $r_{0}$ across the three
mechanisms is 2.52$\times$, beyond the 2$\times$ threshold set in advance,
driven entirely by PIE: the CoDel family agrees to 0.49 against 0.42. The three disciplines differ in their
\texttt{target}/\texttt{interval} ratio as well as in mechanism, so the spread
is confounded; the functional form transfers, the constant does not.

\caption{Benefit of adaptation against $r=\texttt{target}/\mathrm{RTT}$ (left)
and against path RTT alone (right). The same measurements collapse onto one

\end{figure}

\paragraph{What it means in practice.}
Published guidance places a correctly configured deployment at $r$ between
0.05 and 0.10. Half the available benefit arrives only at
$r=\LawHalfBenefit$, five to ten times outside that envelope. Adaptation is
therefore a remedy for misconfiguration rather than an improvement on correct
configuration, and the ratio is computable from two numbers an operator
already has. Where $r$ is small, the correct action is to replace the
discipline rather than tune it.

%% =====================================================================
\section{Threats to validity}

A single 10\,Mbit/s bottleneck in namespaces on one kernel is not a
datacentre, and the ceiling $B$ should be expected to move with link rate and
flow count; the dimensionless form is the portable claim, the constants are
not. Three repetitions per cell give wide intervals, which is why the law is
fitted across \LawCells\ cells rather than rested on any one. A second
dimensionless group, \texttt{target} against the serialisation time of one
MTU, was pre-registered and failed: benefit peaks at intermediate values
rather than being floored, so $r$ alone does not close the problem.

\section{Conclusion}

The question of whether runtime parameter adaptation of an AQM is worth doing
has a quantitative answer, and it is a dimensionless one. Benefit follows a
logistic curve in $r=\texttt{target}/\mathrm{RTT}$ with a ceiling of
\LawCeiling\,\% and half-benefit at $r=\LawHalfBenefit$, invariant across a
16-fold change in RTT and transferring in form to two other AQM mechanisms.
Since correctly configured deployments sit an order of magnitude below the
half-benefit point, the honest reading is that adaptation repairs
misconfiguration, and that replacing the discipline is worth more than tuning
it. The contribution is the predicate, not the mechanism, together with the
apparatus needed to establish it: a sham-controller condition, separated
sparse and bulk latencies, eight alternative disciplines under one bottleneck,
and thresholds fixed before the measurements were taken.
