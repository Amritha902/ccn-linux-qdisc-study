\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Linux ships \texttt{fq\_codel} as its default queue discipline with four
parameters fixed at configuration time. Whether adapting them while traffic
runs is worth doing has been answered both ways, and neither answer states
when the other holds. Across 210 runs spanning three workloads, eight
queue disciplines and path round-trip times from 2 to 200\,ms, the benefit is
governed not by path RTT but by the dimensionless ratio
$r=\texttt{target}/\mathrm{RTT}$, following a logistic curve with half of its
ceiling reached at $r=\LawHalfBenefit$ ($R^{2}=\LawRsq$). The form holds
across a 16-fold change in RTT and transfers to two further AQM mechanisms,
but the ceiling does not: holding $r$ at 1 while sweeping link rate 25-fold
moves the benefit from \RateBenefitMin\ to \RateBenefitMax\,\%. Published
guidance places a correctly configured deployment at $r$ between 0.05 and
0.10, five to ten times below the half-benefit point, making adaptation a
remedy for misconfiguration rather than an improvement on correct
configuration. Flow queueing with a delay target cuts tail latency 99.0\,\%
below an unmanaged queue at indistinguishable goodput, and CAKE, which needs
no controller, beats the adapted system at every RTT tested. All hypotheses and
thresholds were registered before the data existed.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{active queue management; bufferbloat; \texttt{fq\_codel}; CoDel;
dimensionless scaling law; runtime parameter adaptation; Linux traffic
control; pre-registered evaluation}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Bufferbloat is the inflation of queueing delay by buffers larger than any
control loop requires, with throughput left unaffected\cite{gettys2012}. The
Linux response is \texttt{fq\_codel}, which applies Deficit Round Robin across
roughly a thousand flow queues and runs an independent CoDel instance in
each\cite{rfc8290}. Four parameters govern it and none changes once set:
\texttt{target} (5\,ms by default), \texttt{interval} (100\,ms),
\texttt{limit} and \texttt{quantum}.

Adapting those parameters while traffic runs has obvious appeal and clear
precedent. Adaptive RED adjusts a maximum drop probability by an
additive-increase multiplicative-decrease rule\cite{ared2001}; Ye and Leung
derive stability conditions for CoDel and adapt its \texttt{interval}
analytically\cite{yeleung2020}; DESiRED adapts an AQM's delay target in P4
using deep reinforcement learning and in-band telemetry\cite{desired2024}.
Runtime adaptation of a delay target is therefore not novel, and no claim of
novelty is made for it here.

Three families of queue discipline bear on this. Random Early Detection
opened the field by dropping with a probability derived from average queue
length\cite{red1993}, and its sensitivity to configuration is what prompted
Adaptive RED. PIE replaced queue length with a delay estimate inside a
proportional-integral controller\cite{rfc8033}. CoDel abandoned queue length
altogether, dropping when the minimum sojourn time over a sliding interval
exceeds a target\cite{nichols2012,rfc8289}; its authors argue that the two
parameters are expressed relative to path round-trip time by construction and
therefore need no tuning. That claim is qualitative and has not, as far as we
are aware, been given a boundary: it holds for a deployment whose target is
scaled to its path and says nothing about one whose is not.
Equation~\ref{eq:law} supplies the boundary, which is the contribution here.
The mechanism is old and the direction of the effect is unsurprising; what was
missing is the number at which it begins to matter. Beyond \texttt{fq\_codel}, CAKE
integrates shaping, flow queueing and host fairness in one
discipline\cite{cake2018}, FQ-PIE is the corresponding combination for
PIE\cite{fqpie2019}, and L4S standardises a low-latency service class built on
a dual-queue coupled AQM\cite{rfc9330,rfc9332}. All of these except L4S,
whose dual-queue structure needs endpoint support this testbed does not
provide, are measured here against one identical bottleneck, alongside an
unmanaged queue and SFQ.

Among systems that adapt at runtime, QueuePilot learns a marking policy for
small buffers by reinforcement learning\cite{queuepilot2023} and AQM-LLM
distils a language model into a marking controller\cite{aqmllm2025}.
Toopchinezhad and Ahmadi survey the machine-learning AQM literature and
observe that heuristic schemes require careful parameter adjustment, which
limits their applicability in deployment\cite{mlaqm2025}. SCRR revisits
fair-queueing scheduling for modern link rates\cite{scrr2025}, and Ray et
al.\ characterise how the presence of an AQM distorts speed-test
measurement\cite{ray2025}. Table~\ref{tab:systems} places the present work
against this line. Each of these systems reports that its adaptation improved
something, and each adapts a single quantity; none states the conditions under
which that adaptation is worth running, which is the gap addressed here.

What that body of work does not supply is the condition under which adaptation
pays. Systems report gains, independent evaluations report none, and neither
camp states where the boundary between the two lies. RFC 7567 meanwhile
recommends active queue management that requires no per-flow
configuration\cite{rfc7567}, which makes a null result for adaptation a
reportable outcome rather than a failed experiment. Two pieces of apparatus
are missing throughout as well. A controller that merely occupies the
processor is hard to distinguish from one that helps unless a third arm runs
the same code and applies nothing; and the sparse probe traffic used to
measure latency is usually averaged into the bulk flows, even where the
discipline under test was designed to treat those two populations
differently.

This study supplies the missing condition and the apparatus needed to
establish it. The condition turns out to be a dimensionless ratio rather than
any absolute quantity, which is what allows it to be stated once and applied
to deployments of different speed and distance.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

Every value reported here is generated from the committed measurement logs by
the analysis scripts; none is entered by hand. Unless stated otherwise,
figures describe the steady workload at a 20\,ms base path RTT over a
10\,Mbit/s bottleneck, with means taken over three independent runs and
uncertainties given as 95\,\% confidence intervals.

\subsection{Comparison of the four measured outputs across all systems}

Figure~\ref{fig:main} sets out the four outputs the study measures, side by
side, for all ten configurations. Tail latency of the sparse probe and mean
latency of the bulk flows are reported separately throughout, because
flow-queueing disciplines privilege sparse flows by construction and a single
averaged latency conceals that. Queue occupancy is the mechanism those
latencies arise from, and goodput is the quantity a latency improvement might
have been bought with.

The dominant effect is the presence of flow queueing with a delay target, not
the tuning of one. An unmanaged first-in-first-out queue reaches
\PfifoProbeRttPninetyfiveSteady\,ms at the 95th percentile against
\FqCodelProbeRttPninetyfiveSteady\,ms for static \texttt{fq\_codel}, a
99.0\,\% reduction ($p<0.001$), while goodput is statistically
indistinguishable between them (\PfifoGoodputSteady\ against
\FqCodelGoodputSteady\,Mbit/s, $p=0.20$). The goodput panel is drawn from zero
precisely so that this can be seen: every system delivers the same throughput,
so the latency ordering across three orders of magnitude is a property of
queue management and not a rate trade.

Fair queueing on its own is not sufficient. SFQ isolates flows but applies no
delay target, and its bulk queues sit at their 127-packet limit throughout, so
its sparse probe reads \SfqProbeRttMeanSteady\,ms while the bulk flows sharing
the same link read \SfqBulkRttMeanSteady\,ms. The scheduler and the AQM
perform distinct jobs and both are required.

The three \texttt{fq\_codel} arms, static, sham and adapted, are not separable
from one another at this scale on any of the four outputs, which is the first
indication that parameter tuning is a second-order effect.

\subsection{Effect of adaptation, parameter by parameter}

Figure~\ref{fig:allparams} gives every measured quantity against every system
in one view, with each row's rank shown as colour and each row's true range
printed beside it. The rank encoding makes within-row ordering legible across
quantities whose units differ by orders of magnitude; the range column is
there so that a row with strong colour contrast and no meaningful difference,
such as goodput or fairness at 1.01$\times$, is not read as a result.

Under the steady workload the largest movement the controller produces is in
mean queue occupancy, from \FqCodelBacklogSteady\ to
\FqCodelAcapeBacklogSteady\ packets. That is a 9.6\,\% reduction as a point
estimate, but its 95\,\% confidence interval spans $\pm1.00$ packets and so
covers zero, and the same is true of the other three outputs. Three
repetitions per arm is not a design that can resolve small effects: at the
seed-to-seed variation observed here it separates a benefit of about
\RateMDE\,\% from zero, so these null results bound the effect rather than
establish its absence. The sham arm
narrows what the null can be attributed to: the static and sham arms also lie
within each other's intervals on all four outputs, so the controller's
computational cost is not detectable either, and the absence of an effect is
not the cost of running the loop masking a benefit. Under the staged workload,
built specifically to exercise adaptation by varying the flow count from two
to twenty-four during the run, nothing separates there either, and the
adjustments are seen to lag the transitions they respond to. Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
returns to why a null result is expected at this operating point.

The comparison that matters for practice is with CAKE, which requires no
controller, no telemetry and no model of the queue. It reaches
\CakeProbeRttPninetyfiveSteady\,ms at the 95th percentile against
\FqCodelAcapeProbeRttPninetyfiveSteady\,ms for the adapted system. Replacing
the discipline is worth more than tuning it.

\subsection{A scaling law in \texttt{target}/RTT}

The results above are taken at a single path RTT. Sweeping the base RTT from 2
to 200\,ms while holding the configured \texttt{target} fixed shows that the
benefit of adaptation is not a function of path RTT at all, but of the
dimensionless ratio $r=\texttt{target}/\mathrm{RTT}$. Fitting the reduction
in bulk-flow RTT, $b$, over \LawCells\ cells gives
%
\begin{equation}
b(r) \;=\; \frac{B}{1 + \left(r_{0}/r\right)^{k}},
\qquad
B = \LawCeiling\,\%,\quad
r_{0} = \LawHalfBenefit,\quad
k = \LawSteepness,
\label{eq:law}
\end{equation}
%
with $R^{2}=\LawRsq$, where $B$ is the ceiling, $r_{0}$ the ratio at which
half of it is reached and $k$ the sharpness of the transition. Three
parameters over \LawCells\ cells will fit well whatever the shape, so
$R^{2}$ is not the figure to judge this by. Refitting with one cell held out
and asked to predict it, the form above gives a mean absolute error of
\LawLooLogistic\ percentage points, against \LawLooSimple\ for the same
saturating form with the exponent fixed at one and \LawLooStep\ for a step
that is flat below a threshold and flat above it. The third parameter earns
its place out of sample, not merely in fit.
Figure~\ref{fig:law} shows the fit, and shows the same measurements plotted
against path RTT alone, where they do not collapse.

Hypotheses and decision thresholds were committed to a public repository
before any of these data were collected, and three of the four held. A
crossover exists: benefit remains under 1.5\,\% and is not statistically
significant below $r=0.25$, and reaches 16.4 to \LawPeakBenefit\,\% at
$r\ge1$. The curve is invariant to path RTT at fixed ratio: cells at 5, 20 and
80\,ms with $r=1$ give 18.4, 17.6 and 16.4\,\%, a spread of 2.0 percentage
points against a threshold of 5 set in advance. Leave-one-out prediction of
the interior ratios has a mean absolute error of 1.4 percentage points, worst
case 3.5. The functional form also transfers to mechanisms the fit never saw:
refitted independently over the cells each mechanism has in common, plain
\texttt{codel} places $r_{0}$ at 0.42 against \texttt{fq\_codel}'s 0.49, and
PIE, which shares nothing with CoDel beyond possessing a delay target, places
it at 1.06, with $R^{2}$ of 0.978 and 0.992 respectively. The 0.49 here is the
same quantity as the \LawHalfBenefit\ of Equation~\ref{eq:law}, refitted on
the seven cells common to all three mechanisms rather than the full
\LawCells; the small difference is the cost of the smaller fitting set and
not a second estimate.

One pre-registered test failed and is reported as such. The spread in $r_{0}$
across the three mechanisms is 2.52$\times$, beyond the 2$\times$ threshold
set in advance. It is driven entirely by PIE, the CoDel family agreeing to
0.49 against 0.42, and the three disciplines differ in their
\texttt{target}/\texttt{interval} ratio as well as in mechanism, so the
measurement is confounded. The functional form transfers; the constant does
not.

\subsection{The ratio fixes the shape, not the ceiling}\label{sec:rate}

The sweep above varies path RTT at a fixed 10\,Mbit/s bottleneck, so it
establishes that $r$ governs the benefit along the RTT dimension and says
nothing about the other one. A separate campaign supplies it: static and
adapted pairs at \RateMin, 5, 10, 20 and \RateMax\,Mbit/s, three
repetitions each, all on a 5\,ms path with the 5\,ms default
\texttt{target}. The ratio is therefore pinned at $r=1$ in every cell, and
any variation in benefit is variation in the ceiling itself.

Table~\ref{tab:rate} gives the result, and it is not invariant. Benefit
ranges from \RateBenefitMin\ to \RateBenefitMax\,\%, a spread of
\RateSpread\ percentage points, against the 5-point threshold this study
used to call the RTT comparison consistent. Every cell is individually
significant, so this is a real dependence and not sampling noise. It is also
not monotonic: benefit peaks near $s\approx\RatePeakS$ and falls away at both
ends, where $s$ is the configured target measured in units of the time one
maximum-size packet takes to leave the interface.

This is the pre-registered second dimensionless group, and reporting it is
what the earlier failure of that hypothesis amounts to in practice. The
consequence for Equation~\ref{eq:law} is specific: $r$ determines where the
transition sits and how sharp it is, and the fitted $B$ of \LawCeiling\,\%
is the ceiling at 10\,Mbit/s rather than a constant of the mechanism. An
operator reading the predicate should take $r$ as telling them whether
adaptation can help and the rate column as telling them roughly how much.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

Equation~\ref{eq:law} has a direct operational reading. Published guidance
places a correctly configured deployment at $r$ between 0.05 and 0.10, while
half the available benefit arrives only at $r=\LawHalfBenefit$, five to ten
times outside that envelope. This also accounts for the null result of
Section~\ref{sec:results}. Those measurements were taken at the Linux default
\texttt{target} of 5\,ms over a 20\,ms path, which is $r=0.25$, and
Equation~\ref{eq:law} puts the expected benefit there at 2.9\,\% against a
measured 1.4\,\%, which three repetitions per cell cannot resolve. The controller was not failing in those
measurements; it was being asked to work at a ratio where the law says there
is little to recover. On a correctly configured link, therefore, there
is almost nothing for parameter adaptation to recover, and the honest
description of what such a controller does is that it repairs
misconfiguration. The ratio is computable from two numbers an operator already
has, the configured target and a path RTT estimate, so the predicate can be
evaluated before any controller is deployed.

Where the ratio is small, the measurements point to a different action.
Switching from \texttt{fq\_codel} to CAKE is worth more at every RTT tested
than tuning \texttt{fq\_codel}'s four parameters, and it requires no runtime
machinery at all. That the largest available improvement comes from a
configuration change rather than from control is not a comfortable result for
an adaptive system, but it is what the measurements show.

Several limits bound these claims. Network namespaces on one kernel are not a
datacentre, and the ceiling $B$ does move with link rate, as
Section~\ref{sec:rate} measures directly: the dimensionless form is the
portable claim and the fitted constants are not. Flow count was not varied and
should be expected to matter in the same way. Three repetitions per cell
give wide intervals, which is why the law is fitted across \LawCells\ cells
rather than rested on any single one. The second dimensionless group of
Section~\ref{sec:rate} was pre-registered with the prediction that benefit
would be floored below unity; it instead peaks at intermediate values, so $r$
alone does not close the problem and a complete predicate needs both groups. And
the cross-mechanism transfer established the form but not the constant, as
described above.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Testbed}

Three Linux network namespaces model a client, a router and a server,
connected by virtual Ethernet pairs. The router carries \texttt{netem} for
base propagation delay and a token-bucket filter at 10\,Mbit/s as its root
queue discipline, with the discipline under test installed as the child of
that shaper on the egress interface facing the server. Placing the bottleneck
there puts it on the data path. An earlier two-node arrangement placed it so
that it shaped the reverse direction, and every queue measurement taken that
way described the acknowledgement path rather than the data path.

\subsection{Queue disciplines evaluated}

Nine configurations are compared under an identical bottleneck:
\texttt{pfifo} with no AQM, SFQ, RED, \texttt{codel}, PIE, FQ-PIE,
CAKE\cite{cake2018}, static \texttt{fq\_codel}, and \texttt{fq\_codel} under
the controller described below. CAKE is configured \texttt{besteffort}
without its own bandwidth setting so that the token bucket remains the only
shaper and every discipline meets the same bottleneck. A tenth arm, the sham
controller, polls at the controller's cadence and executes the same
classification and prediction code but applies no parameter change; it exists
to separate the controller's computational cost from the effect of its
decisions.

\subsection{Controller}

At each control tick the controller reads the queue discipline's own
statistics together with per-flow state maintained by an eBPF program attached
at the \texttt{tc} clsact egress hook\cite{ebpfqdisc2023}, classifies a congestion regime from
drop rate and backlog, and estimates a trajectory by least squares over a
ten-sample window. At each adjustment opportunity it applies an
additive-increase multiplicative-decrease rule taken from Adaptive
RED\cite{ared2001}, with $\beta=0.9$ on decrease. Observation and adjustment
run on separated timescales an order of magnitude apart, following the
two-timescale stochastic approximation framework\cite{borkar1997}.

Every bound, step size and regime threshold is expressed relative to the
configured \texttt{target} rather than in absolute milliseconds. Expressed
absolutely, a controller started against an 80\,ms target would take steps
sixteen times smaller in relative terms than one started against 5\,ms, and
the ratio dependence reported in Equation~\ref{eq:law} would then be partly an
artefact of the controller's constants rather than a property of the queue.

Flow-map contents are read through the \texttt{bpf(2)} system call directly
rather than by invoking an external utility per sample, which reduces the cost
of one control tick from 3.17 to 0.709\,s and avoids introducing a second
process whose scheduling would confound the latency comparison the sham arm
exists to isolate.

\subsection{Measurements}

Three latencies are recorded separately: a sparse ICMP probe for the latency
a thin flow experiences, the smoothed round-trip time of the bulk TCP flows as
reported by \texttt{iperf3}, and a queue-delay proxy computed from
instantaneous backlog and the configured rate. They are not interchangeable,
and the ratio between the sparse and the bulk latency ranges from 1.0 to 9.6
across the systems tested. Goodput is taken from \texttt{iperf3}'s received
byte count and flow fairness is reported as Jain's index\cite{jain1984}.

\subsection{Experimental design and statistics}

Three workloads are used: a steady bulk load of eight TCP CUBIC\cite{cubic2008}
flows, a staged load whose flow count varies from two to twenty-four during
the run so that the congestion regime changes, and a mixed load adding sparse
constant-rate UDP probes alongside bulk traffic. Each cell is repeated three
times as an independent run. Differences between conditions are assessed with
Welch's $t$-test, which does not assume equal group variances. Two-sided
$p$-values are computed exactly from Student's $t$ distribution through the
regularised incomplete beta function rather than by normal approximation,
which at three samples per group materially overstates significance.

\subsection{Platform}

Experiments ran on Linux 6.12.48 under hardware-assisted virtualisation. The
kernel is unmodified upstream source; the only departure from a distribution
build is the configuration, which compiles every evaluated queue discipline in
rather than as a module and enables BPF Type Format. No kernel code was
patched, which is what \emph{stock Linux} in the title refers to: the
disciplines under test are the ones a deployment would get.
